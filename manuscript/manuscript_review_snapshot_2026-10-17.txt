[1; t.0] Distinct speech functions are spatially organized within the human insula.

[76; t.0] Introduction

[89; t.0] Human speech depends on coordinated activity across a distributed brain network. Within this network, the insula has long been implicated by clinical and physiological evidence. lesions and electrical stimulation to can disrupt speech, while neuroimaging and intracranial recordings reveal its engagement across a range of speech tasks. Despite this extensive evidence, studies have assigned the insula diverse from speech perception to motor planning and sometimes conflicting functional roles, and some even denying the insular contribution. How these speech contributions are organized within the human insula remains unresolved.

[722; t.0] Studies of the insula have yielded diverse and sometimes conflicting accounts of its contribution to speech. Clinical and neuroimaging evidence has long supported a role in speech motor control, including associations between insular damage and motor speech disorders, stimulation-induced speech arrest, and activation during articulatory tasks. Responses to heard and self-generated speech have also motivated accounts emphasizing sensory feedback monitoring. Neuroimaging studies further implicate the insula in verbal working memory, showing sensitivity to phonological memory load and activity during the maintenance and manipulation of verbal information. However, specific functional interpretations remain contested. Limited pre-articulatory activity and a lack of sensitivity to articulatory complexity in some studies have challenged the proposed role of the insula in articulatory planning. These findings leave unresolved how the proposed motor, sensory and cognitive contributions relate to one another within the insula. Understanding how these contributions are organized across insular populations and expressed as speech unfolds is central to developing a coherent account of insular function.

[1932; t.0] To determine how speech-related functions are organized within the human insula, we obtained large-scale intracranial EEG recordings during a battery of speech and language tasks. These tasks separated sensory input from speech output and varied input modality and response demands, allowing us to examine insular responses across processing stages and behavioral contexts. Using a data-driven decomposition approach, we identified sensory, sustained and motor components with distinct temporal dynamics and partially overlapping spatial distributions. These components differed in their sensitivity to input modality and behavioral context. Complementary analyses linked this functional differentiation to speech-related representations, behavior and activity in the broader speech network. Together, these findings reveal a spatially differentiated functional architecture underlying the insula’s contributions across multiple stages of speech.

[2881; t.0] Results

[2889; t.0] Human insula is extensively recruited during speech

[2944; t.0] To characterize insular activity during speech, we obtained intracranial recordings from 89 participants performing delayed speech tasks. Participants heard a speech stimulus and, after a delay, produced a verbal response following a go cue (Fig. 1a). This task structure separated auditory input from speech output, allowing us to examine activity during stimulus presentation, the intervening delay and speech production. Recordings comprised [N] bipolar sites across the brain, including 464 sites distributed across the left and right insula (Fig. 1b).

[3501; t.0] Of the 464 insular sites, 348 (75%) showed a significant increase in high-gamma activity relative to baseline during at least one task phase (cluster-based permutation tests, [corrected P threshold]; Fig. 1c). Responsive sites were distributed across both hemispheres (not significant for lateralization, see SIxxx). An example recording and the population-averaged response illustrated insular activity across stimulus presentation, the delay and speech production (Fig. 1d,e; population average, n = [N] sites). Insular sites accounted for 8.8% of all speech-responsive recording sites in the sampled brain regions, comparable to some of the canonical speech regions like STG, IFG, and SMC (Fig. 1f). These observations establish widespread insular recruitment during the task and motivate examining how its responses are organized across time and space.

[4359; t.0] Insular speech activity comprises sensory, sustained and motor components

[4435; t.0] To determine how this widespread insular activity was organized, we applied non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) to high-gamma responses during delayed repetition. For each speech-responsive insular site, trial- and task-averaged responses from the stimulus, delay and response epochs were concatenated along the time dimension (Fig. 2a). We compared solutions containing two to six components. Cosine silhouette scores were similar for the two- and three-component solutions but declined markedly with additional components (Fig. 2b). We retained three components to capture greater response diversity while preserving separation between groups. Assigning each electrode to the component with the largest spatial weight defined three populations, which showed separation in principal-component space, with some overlap (Fig. 2c).

[5269; t.0] The population-averaged responses exhibited distinct temporal profiles: one component responded predominantly during stimulus presentation, another maintained activity during the delay, and a third showed increased activity during speech production (Fig. 2d). We termed these populations sensory, sustained and motor, respectively, based on their temporal profiles. Individual-electrode responses further illustrated these temporal preferences and the variability within each population (Fig. 2e).

[5767; t.0] Mapping the component weights revealed partially overlapping spatial distributions with distinct anatomical preferences (Fig. 2f). The sensory component was most prominent in the posterior insula, the sustained component in dorsal anterior regions, and the motor component in the mid-insula. These findings linked distinct temporal response profiles to spatial differentiation within the insula. We next used the component-based electrode assignments to examine differences in task sensitivity, speech-related information and associations with reaction time.

[6328; t.0] Insular speech activities are dissociated by input modality and upcoming goals

[6409; t.0] To study the functional properties of these components, we first study the response profile of taking input information. Compared to the “sound-vocal” repetition circuit, we also collect data fron picture naming conditions, where participants were asked to name a picture item after they see them. Results showed that Sensory and motor components respond primarily to sound input, while the sustain components showed significant activity to both modality. These electrodes that respond to image were mainly distributed on the bilateral dorsal anterior insula. 

[6971; t.0] We next asked how these populations responded to different behavioral demands. We compared auditory repetition with a lexical-decision condition, in which participants heard a word or pseudoword (for example, “bagel” or “[pseudoword]”) and, after a delay, verbally reported whether it was a real word. Both conditions involved auditory input and verbal output, but lexical decision additionally required adding more cognitive effort to evaluate lexical status and selecting an appropriate response. During the delay, sustained populations showed greater activity during lexical decision than during repetition [statistics; Fig. 3[X]], whereas sensory and motor populations showed relatively little difference between conditions. This enhancement supports the involvement of sustained activity in the dorsal anterior insula in the cognitive demands of speech tasks.

[7839; t.0] Insula activities contain phoneme-articulatory to lexical information

[7911; t.0] We next asked what speech-related information was carried by these functionally differentiated insular populations. We first examined fundamental phonetic information by grouping speech sounds according to their articulatory features. Trials in the phoneme sequence task were labeled according to the initial phoneme as high vowels, low vowels, labial consonants or dorsal consonants, distinguishing vowel height and consonant place of articulation (Methods). Using cross-validated linear classifiers, we found that this articulatory-category information could be decoded from each insular population at different task stages. Classification exceeded chance during stimulus presentation in sensory and motor populations, during the delay in sustained populations, and during speech production in motor populations (Fig. 4a,b; chance, 25%; permutation tests, P < 0.05). These results show that insular populations carried phonetic information across task stages, including during the interval between auditory input and speech output. Together, these findings support the involvement of the insula in representing fundamental phonetic features across speech perception, the intervening delay and production.

[9118; t.0] We then asked whether insular activity also carried higher-level speech information such as lexical-semantic beyond phonetic features. To do this, we used data in the lexical decision task. We trained time-resolved linear classifiers to distinguish words from pseudowords during repetition and lexical decision. Results showed that sustain group is the only component that can achieve above-chance decoding of lexical status during the delay in both tasks [statistics; Fig. 4c]. Sensory also achieve significant decoding during decision condition, but thats due to saying yes and no. 

[9703; t.0] To examine whether this information generalized across task demands, we trained classifiers on repetition trials and tested them on lexical-decision trials. Cross-task decoding exceeded chance during the delay [statistics; Fig. 4d], supporting a shared component of lexical-status information across the two conditions. Together, these findings show that sustained insular populations carried lexical information during the interval preceding a verbal response.

[10165; t.0] Together, these results link the functional differentiation of insular populations to the representation of speech content at both phonetic and lexical levels. Decodable information was present during perception, the intervening delay and production, with sustained populations carrying both phonetic-category and lexical-status information during the delay. These findings support insular involvement in representing speech content across multiple stages of speech processing.

[12600; t.0] Insular response profiles recur across the broader speech network

[12668; t.0] Finally, we asked how does this insula activity is related to the brain-wide speech network. Due to the sparsity of intracranial sampling, we chose to project the three temporal components derived from insula to the significant activation on the cortex. For each electrode’s significant activity on the cortex, we calculated the spatial weight based on non-negative least squares (Fig. 5a). The weight denotes how activity of each electrode resembles the template of insula (Fig 5b).

The sensory profile was prominent in posterior superior temporal and adjacent temporal regions, whereas the sustained profile was expressed across frontal regions, including the middle frontal gyrus and orbitofrontal cortex. The motor profile was prominent in precentral and postcentral regions.

[13693; t.0] Discussion

[13704; t.0] Here we used large scale intracranial recordings across speech and language tasks to examine how speech-related processes are organized within the human insula. We identified sensory, sustained and motor response profiles with partially overlapping distributions centered on posterior, dorsal anterior and middle insular regions, respectively. Task comparisons revealed differences in their sensitivity to input modality and response demands, supporting functional distinctions among these temporal profiles. Insular populations also carried phonetic and lexical information across task stages, and their activity predicted variation in speech reaction time. Together, these findings indicate that the insula contributes to sensory, cognitive and motor aspects of speech through populations with distinct temporal and spatial properties, providing an account of how its diverse speech-related roles are organized.

[14619; t.0] The spatial organization of speech-related functions within the insula is consistent with cortico-cortical connectivity with the wider brain. Both CCEP and structural connectivity evidence has shown similar evidence that the anterior insula is more connected to the frontal brain, mid to the motor and posterior to the temporal. These connectivity pattern is agreed with the similarity pattern we found in Fig 6. These difference in spatial connectivity leads to the diverse functional profile for insula, as it showed a sensorimotor to cognitive gradient from posterior to anterior section.

[15211; t.0] This spatial organization provides a framework to reconcile the century-long controversey on diverse accounts of insular contributions to speech. Speech rapidly recruits multiple processes, making their separation difficult when temporal resolution, spatial localization or anatomical sampling is limited. In this paper, we used large-scale ieeg provide a unique tool to study this. These methodological constraints, together with differences in task demands, may have emphasized different aspects of insular function. In particular, sparse and weak pre-articulatory activity in previous intracranial recordings and fmri challenged the proposed contribution of the anterior insula to speech planning. However, here we showed that, anterior insula showed pre-articulatory activity, which contains information of articulatory movement and  predicts reaction time. Complementary evidence comes from recordings showing that tongue- and mouth-related insular activity precedes movement onset, together with stimulation demonstrating effector-specific connectivity with primary motor cortex. Beside this, single unit recording also showed that insula reacts to simple sound input from posterior to sparsely on anterior. Together, these findings suggest that sensory, preparatory and motor contributions coexist within the insula, with regional preferences that become apparent when activity is resolved across task stages and behavioral conditions.

[16655; t.0] The insula’s contribution to speech can be understood within a broader organization in which language-selective regions work alongside domain-general systems that support task performance. Such an organization allows regions with broad functional roles to contribute to language according to behavioral demands. Our sustained populations illustrate how these contributions may involve specific speech content. These populations responded to both auditory and visual inputs and showed stronger delay-period activity during lexical decision than repetition. During the same interval, their activity carried phonetic and lexical-status information, with lexical-status decoding generalizing from repetition to decision trials. Thus, sensitivity to task demands was accompanied by representations of the information being processed. This combination is consistent with the action-mode network framework, which places anterior insular regions within a system supporting goal maintenance, action selection and control. Viewed in this context, sustained insular activity may help maintain speech-related information for use in an upcoming verbal response. This interpretation provides a concrete way for mechanisms shared across behavioral domains to participate in speech: by maintaining relevant content as the demands on its use change.

[18800; t.0] Speech task

[18812; t.0] Participants performed speech and language tasks with a common delayed-response structure. On each trial, an auditory or visual stimulus was presented, followed by a delay. Participants withheld their verbal response until a go cue. Task-specific sample sizes, recording-site coverage and contributions to individual analyses are summarized in Supplementary Table [X].

[19181; t.0] Phoneme sequence task. Participants listened to three-phoneme sequences and repeated them aloud after a delay. The stimulus set comprised 52 sequences with consonant–vowel–consonant or vowel–consonant–vowel structures (for example, “gab” and “uga”). Each trial began with a visual “Listen” cue, followed by an auditory presentation of the target sequence. After a delay of approximately 1–1.25 s following stimulus presentation, a visual “Speak” cue instructed participants to repeat the sequence in the same order. Participants withheld their response until the speak cue. Task-specific participant numbers, recording-site coverage and contributions to individual analyses are provided in Supplementary Table [X].

[19896; t.0] Delayed repetition and lexical decision task Participants listened to spoken words or pseudowords and either repeated the stimulus or judged its lexical status. Each trial began with a visual cue indicating the response requirement (“Repeat” or “Yes/No”), followed by the auditory stimulus. After a delay of approximately 1–1.25 s, a visual “Speak” cue instructed participants to respond aloud. In repetition trials, participants reproduced the stimulus; in lexical-decision trials, they responded “yes” for words and “no” for pseudowords. Participants withheld their response until the speak cue.

[20494; t.0] Picture naming and word repetition task. Participants were presented with pictures, spoken words referring to the same set of 4 items (star/apple/umbrella/?). Two kinds of condition was included, passive and repetition. A cue at the beginning of each trial indicated whether a verbal response was required. In response trials, participants named the picture, repeated the spoken word or read the written word aloud after a go cue. Stimulus presentation was followed by a delay of approximately 1–1.25 s before the cue. In passive trials, participants viewed or listened to the stimulus without making a verbal response. The auditory and picture conditions provided corresponding target words across input modalities.

[21211; t.0] Single-word repetition task. Participants listened to individual spoken words and repeated them aloud after a delay. The single-word stimuli comprised “heat”, “hoot”, “hot” and “hut”, which shared their initial and final consonants and differed in the intervening vowel. Repetition trials began with a visual “Listen” cue, followed by the auditory stimulus. After a delay of approximately 0.75–1 s, a visual “Speak” cue instructed participants to repeat the word aloud.

[21718; t.0] Intracranial EEG signals were acquired using [recording system and manufacturer] at a sampling rate of [sampling rate] Hz. Speech responses were recorded using [audio recording system], and task events and audio recordings were synchronized with neural signals using [synchronization procedure]. [22331; t.0] High-gamma activity was extracted over 70–200 Hz using a filter bank and Hilbert transformation. Task epochs were extracted with an additional 0.5 s on either side of each analysis interval to reduce edge effects during filtering. [22795; t.0] Neural activity was aligned to stimulus onset, stimulus offset (delay), and articulation onset, as appropriate for each analysis. High-gamma amplitude was standardized separately for each channel using the mean and standard deviation calculated across trials and time points within a 500-ms baseline preceding the condition-specific trial-start or instruction event. The same baseline estimates were applied to all task phases within a condition. After standardization, trial–channel epochs were marked as missing if their maximum absolute amplitude exceeded the channel-wise mean absolute amplitude by more than 15 standard deviations, calculated across trials and time points. This procedure excluded the affected channel’s entire epoch while retaining data from other channels on the same trial.

Tests were performed on amplitude envelopes after z-score normalization for each electrode. Adjacent time points exceeding the cluster-forming threshold of [threshold] were grouped into temporal clusters. Cluster significance was evaluated against the permutation distribution of the maximum cluster duration, using a cluster-level threshold of [threshold]. For preprocessing-level channel selection, recording sites were retained if at least one time point within the specified phase window satisfied both the cluster criterion and a pointwise P < 0.05.

[25741; t.0] Data-driven decomposition and spatial organization

[25792; t.0] We used non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) to identify temporal response components in insular high-gamma activity. Only data from repetition conditions at insular recording sites exhibiting significant high-gamma responses were included. For each site, activity was averaged across trials and tasks. Responses were aligned to stimulus onset, stimulus offset and articulation onset, using windows of 0–0.6 s, 0–1 s and 0–1 s, respectively. These windows were chosen to approximate the average durations of the stimulus, delay and utterance. The three epochs were concatenated along the time dimension to form a channel-by-time matrix for NMF.

[26440; t.0] Before decomposition, negative values were set to zero and normalized to unit Euclidean norm, emphasizing temporal response shape rather than absolute amplitude. The resulting matrix was factorized into a non-negative channel-by-component weight matrix and a non-negative component-by-time matrix. NMF was fitted using coordinate descent to minimize squared reconstruction error. We retained the solution with the lowest reconstruction error across 20 initializations.

[26909; t.0] The number of components was selected using consensus clustering across repeated subsamples of recording sites. For each candidate rank from two to six, we fitted NMF to 200 subsamples, each containing 80% of sites selected without replacement. Consensus matrices quantified how often pairs of sites were assigned to the same component when sampled together. We applied average-linkage hierarchical clustering to the corresponding dissimilarity matrices and selected the rank with the highest cophenetic correlation, yielding three components.

[27453; t.0] The three components were termed sensory, sustained and motor based on their temporal waveforms, reflecting stimulus-related responses, activity persisting through the delay, and responses associated with speech production, respectively. To visualize their spatial distributions, electrode coordinates were mapped to a common anatomical template. Component weights were displayed separately on the insular surface using Gaussian smoothing (σ = 5 mm). Vertices farther than 6 mm from the nearest recording site were masked, and maps were displayed above 40% of the maximum density within each component and hemisphere.

[28071; t.0] Decoding

[28080; t.0] We decoded the articulatory category of the initial phoneme during delayed repetition, distinguishing dorsal consonants (/g/, /k/), labial consonants (/b/, /p/, /v/), high vowels (/i/, /u/) and low vowels (/a/, /ae/). Analyses were performed separately for sensory, sustained and motor populations using sites with significant responses in the corresponding task phase. Features comprised all channel-by-time samples within windows of 0–0.5 s after stimulus onset, 0–0.7 s after stimulus offset and 0–1 s after articulation onset.

[28611; t.0] Classification was performed using a linear support vector machine (C = 1) with stratified fivefold cross-validation. Within each training fold, features were standardized and reduced using principal component analysis, retaining components that explained 80% of the variance. The fitted transformations and classifier were then applied to held-out trials. Performance was quantified using balanced accuracy, with a chance level of 25%. Cross-validation was repeated with 30 different partitions to assess stability; reported accuracy and row-normalized confusion matrices were obtained from the first repetition.

[29225; t.0] Statistical significance was assessed against a null distribution generated using 200 label permutations with the same fivefold partition as the first cross-validation repetition. For each permutation, training labels were shuffled within each fold and the classification pipeline was refitted, while test labels remained unchanged. Accuracy was averaged across folds to obtain one null score per permutation. One-sided P values were calculated as the number of null scores greater than or equal to the observed score, plus one, divided by 201. Decoding was considered significant at P < 0.05 for each population–phase combination.

[29857; t.0] We decoded lexical status (words versus pseudowords) separately within sensory, sustained and motor populations during repetition and lexical-decision trials. Single-trial high-gamma activity was pooled across participants by matching stimulus identity and presentation number. Decoding used 300-ms sliding windows advanced in nominal 30-ms steps. Within each window, activity was averaged over five consecutive temporal bins and concatenated across recording sites. Features were standardized using the training data and classified using linear discriminant analysis with automatic covariance shrinkage. We used stratified fivefold cross-validation grouped by stimulus identity, keeping all presentations of each word or pseudoword in the same fold. Decision values were standardized within each test fold before pooling held-out predictions to calculate the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC; chance, 0.5). Time points denote window endpoints.

[30829; t.0] To assess cross-task generalization during the delay, classifiers were trained on repetition trials and tested on lexical-decision trials using recording sites and stimulus presentations shared between conditions. The same stimulus-grouped cross-validation scheme ensured that test items were absent from the training set. Classifiers fitted at each training time window were evaluated at every test time window, yielding a training-time-by-test-time AUC matrix.

[31292; t.0] Null distributions were generated using 5,000 permutations of lexical-status labels across unique stimulus items, with all presentations of an item retaining the same permuted label. Cross-validation partitions were held fixed, and classifiers were refitted and evaluated using the permuted labels. For cross-task decoding, the same label permutation was applied to both conditions. Pointwise significance was assessed using the proportion of null AUC values at least as large as the observed value, with a plus-one correction. Adjacent points with P < 0.05 formed candidate clusters. Sliding-window results were corrected using the maximum temporal cluster length in each permutation. Cross-task matrices used two-dimensional clusters, including diagonal adjacency, with cluster mass defined as the sum of AUC above 0.5. Observed clusters were compared with the corresponding maximum-cluster null distributions at P < 0.05. Correction was performed separately for each population–phase–condition time course or population-specific cross-task matrix.

[32343; t.0] Predict reaction time

[32365; t.0] Reaction time (RT) was defined as the interval between the go cue and articulation onset. Trials RTs shorter than 50 ms or longer than 2s were excluded, and RTs were natural-log-transformed before analysis. Ridge regression models were fitted separately for each participant, task and insular recording site using repetition trials. Features comprised high-gamma amplitude samples within approximately 200-ms windows advanced by three samples at 128 Hz (23.4 ms). Analyses were performed on activity aligned to stimulus offset, the go cue and articulation onset.

[32928; t.0] Prediction was evaluated using cross-validation grouped by stimulus identity, keeping all presentations of an item in the same fold. The outer cross-validation used up to ten folds, depending on the number of available items. Missing features were replaced with training-set medians, and features were standardized using the outer training set. Regularization strength was selected from ten logarithmically spaced values between 10⁻³ and 10³ using up to five inner, item-grouped folds. Performance was quantified as the Pearson correlation between observed log RTs and predictions pooled across held-out trials.

[33540; t.0] Null distributions were generated using 1,000 permutations of trial-level RTs within each outer training fold. Test RTs and cross-validation partitions remained unchanged, and regularization was reselected for each permutation. Adjacent windows with pointwise P ≤ 0.05 formed temporal clusters. Cluster significance was evaluated against the maximum cluster length across all tested sites and epochs within each participant and task. Clusters with corrected P ≤ 0.05 and positive prediction correlations were considered significant.

[34073; t.0] To characterize the direction of the HGA–RT relationship, we selected, for each site and task, the significantly predictive go-aligned window with the highest prediction correlation among windows beginning before the go cue. Mean HGA within this window was correlated with log RT across trials. Electrode-level correlations were summarized by their median within each participant, task and functional population, then across tasks within each participant, and assessed against zero using a one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test. For trial-level displays, window-averaged HGA was standardized within each electrode and averaged across selected electrodes. A complementary permutation analysis shuffled RTs within each participant and task 5,000 times and compared the observed one-sample t statistic of participant-level correlations with its lower-tail null distribution. Selected sites and windows remained fixed during this analysis. Fast and slow trials were defined by a median split of RT within each participant, task and functional population. 

[35124; t.0] Whole-brain projection of insular temporal profiles

[35176; t.0] To examine how insular temporal profiles were expressed across the brain, we fitted high-gamma activity at speech-responsive recording sites using the three temporal components identified by insular NMF. Trial- and task-averaged responses from repetition conditions were concatenated using the same temporal windows and preprocessing as the insular decomposition. The temporal profiles were held fixed, and non-negative least squares was used to estimate their contributions to each electrode’s activity, minimizing the squared reconstruction error. This yielded three non-negative weights per electrode without re-estimating the temporal components.

[35827; t.0] For spatial visualization, projection weights were normalized within each electrode and weighted by the fraction of signal energy explained by the reconstruction. Component specificity was defined relative to the larger contribution of the other two components. Specificity values were mapped onto a common cortical surface using Gaussian spatial averaging within participants, followed by equal-weight averaging across participants. Maps excluded electrodes used to derive the insular templates and displayed regions preferentially expressing each temporal profile. Regional distributions were summarized by electrode counts within these territories.
