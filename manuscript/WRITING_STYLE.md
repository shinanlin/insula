## 3. Writing rules for author review

### R1. Open a new analysis with its specific question or purpose

**Original example: E1.** State what the analysis must distinguish, then introduce the operation. Avoid an opening that merely announces further investigation without naming what remains unknown. A question-led opening is useful at an inferential transition, not mandatory for every paragraph.

**Project application:** after task comparisons establish functional differences, decoding asks what speech information these populations carry; RT asks how their activity relates to behavior. Each transition should make that new question explicit.

### R2. Make the analytical action concrete

**Original example: E2.** Prefer a named operation and its target to a vague declaration that an analysis was conducted. Useful verbs include compare, estimate, classify, decode, predict and fit; choose the verb that describes the actual operation.

**Project application:** distinguish fitting temporal templates to cortical activity from measuring anatomical connectivity. A more impressive-sounding verb must not change what was measured.

### R3. Use short pivot sentences and longer evidence sentences

**Original examples: E2 and E5.** E2 is an eight-word analytical pivot; E5 is an eighteen-word conceptual opening. Their surrounding passages also contain longer sentences carrying conditions, observations and qualifications. The useful habit is variation in sentence function, not uniform brevity.

**Project editing heuristic, not a measured source rule:** inspect a sentence when it introduces several new claims or requires the reader to retain multiple unfinished clauses. Split at a change of inferential job, rather than at an arbitrary word limit. Do not remove necessary task or statistical detail merely to shorten a sentence.

### R4. Give each Results paragraph one inferential destination

**Original example: E3, read with its full paragraph.** The paragraph establishes a spatial question, describes an observation, quantifies it and closes with the supported anatomical conclusion. The sequence is useful because every step serves the same claim.

**Project application:** introduce enough method to understand the comparison, report the relevant result, then state its immediate implication. Do not append several unrelated claims about memory, planning and cognition to one contrast.

### R5. Let the strength and scope of the conclusion follow the evidence

**Original example: E3.** Its anatomical conclusion follows an explicit spatial test. This illustrates a conclusion tied to a measurement; it is not permission to use equally strong language for every result.

**Project application:** use direct language for observed effects and calibrated language for functional interpretations. Decodable lexical status is an observation; semantic processing is a further interpretation. Temporal-profile similarity does not establish connectivity, and a correlation with RT does not establish causal control.

### R6. Use transitions to express a relationship, not merely chronology

**Original examples: E1 and E3.** E1 connects a hypothesis to its test; E3 connects evidence to its consequence. The connective has a specific logical task.

**Project application:** before adding a transition, decide whether the next passage tests an alternative, extends a result, changes scale or draws a conclusion. Do not make every paragraph begin with the same stock phrase. Prefer a substantive bridge over a generic declaration that the next analysis is important.

### R7. Keep Results methods brief; make Methods reproducible

**Original examples: E2 and E4.** E2 gives an analytical operation in a short Results sentence. E4 specifies both a transformation and its normalization scope in Methods; preceding sentences supply the processing sequence.

**Project application:** Results needs the comparison, main analytical principle and outcome. Methods needs inputs, averaging, time windows, normalization, model fitting, validation and null testing wherever applicable. Concision means avoiding duplication, not omitting decisions that affect the result.

### R8. Choose active or passive voice according to the subject that matters

**Original examples: E2 and E4.** Active voice makes an analytical decision direct; passive voice keeps a preprocessing operation focused on the signal. Neither voice is universally preferable.

**Project application:** use the investigators as subject when explaining a choice or test, and the data, participants or model when describing their treatment or behavior. Avoid strings of impersonal nominalizations that obscure the operation.

### R9. Start Discussion with the scientific problem the findings change

**Original example: E5, read with the opening Discussion paragraph.** The opening identifies the functional demand; the paragraph then connects the findings to an account of neural organization. The lesson is the connection between finding and explanation, rather than a mandatory opening formula.

**Project application:** explain how the findings change the account of speech function within the insula. Do not reopen Discussion with a catalogue of six analyses, and do not let the final synthesis narrow the entire paper to sustained activity or AMN.

### R10. Keep technical nouns stable and comparisons explicit

**Original examples: E3 and E4.** The sentences name the objects of the claim or operation directly: response classes, anatomical segregation, HG signals and the task period.

**Project application:** retain consistent names for temporal components, assigned electrode populations, task conditions and decoded labels. Use lexical status for the word–pseudoword distinction. Do not alternate among activity, information, function and mechanism as stylistic synonyms. Prefer a named condition and time window to an ambiguous reference to these responses.

## 4. What to avoid: project choices, not claims that the papers never do it

The source papers do use long sentences, strong interpretive verbs, contrastive framing and checks of alternative explanations. S1 explicitly works through several concerns; S2 and S3 make strong claims about neural organization. We adopt their specificity and evidence-led progression without copying every rhetorical choice.

### R11. Avoid empty emphasis and defensive detours

**Positive original example: E2.** It moves directly to a concrete analysis. This is a model of economy, not evidence that the source avoids defensive reasoning elsewhere.

**Project choice:** remove assertions that a result is interesting, fundamental or important unless the sentence specifies why. Discuss alternative explanations where they materially affect the inference; do not make anticipated objections the organizing principle of every paragraph. Do not portray previous work as disproving all insular contributions when it tested a narrower claim.

### R12. Avoid conclusions broader than the paragraph has earned

**Positive original example: E3.** Its concluding scope matches the spatial analysis immediately preceding it.

**Project choice:** a subsection should end on its specific contribution. Reserve the whole-insula synthesis for places where the evidence has been assembled. Avoid repeatedly concluding that the insula participates in the entire speech process after individual analyses. Likewise, do not equate speech-related information with language exclusivity.

## 5. Section-level implementation for this manuscript

These are applications of the rules above, not additional claims about universal source habits.

- **Introduction (R1, R4, R9):** establish the insula's relevance and the unresolved organizational problem; explain why existing functional accounts remain unintegrated; introduce the study's approach and principal advance. Give the first two paragraphs different jobs.
- **Results (R1–R7, R10):** recruitment → temporal components and spatial distributions → functional comparisons → represented information → behavioral association → brain-wide temporal correspondence. Preserve figure order where it supports this reasoning.
- **Discussion (R5, R9, R12):** interpret the organization, relate it to connectivity, revisit prior functional accounts, discuss speech information within broader functions, and close on the whole insula. Literature should explain findings rather than form a list.
- **Methods (R2, R7, R8, R10):** describe operations in dependency order, with the unit of analysis and statistical reference distribution explicit. Preserve unknown values as placeholders.

## 6. Author review and future-agent use

For manual review, mark any rule **keep**, **revise** or **remove**, and edit its wording directly. All rules are currently proposals; no author approval is implied. The sentence bank is source evidence, not prose to reuse in our manuscript.

Before writing, an agent should:

1. Read this file and the latest manuscript handoff; use the current Google Doc for manuscript content.
2. Identify the paragraph's one main claim, the evidence supporting it and its role in the surrounding argument.
3. Draft original English using the relevant reviewed rules. Do not reproduce source sentences or import the source papers' scientific claims into this project.
4. Check scope, terminology, sentence load and the transition into the next paragraph. State unresolved scientific inputs instead of inventing them.

Treat this as an editable guide, not a checklist to display with every writing response. New author feedback overrides this draft and should be incorporated when requested.
